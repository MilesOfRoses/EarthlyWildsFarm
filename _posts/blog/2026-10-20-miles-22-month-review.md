<p>
Some of my most recent experiences of overt Anti-Blackness have occurred throughout 2025 and the beginning of 2026 in relation to my racial advocacy work within a majority white intentional community called Twin Oaks.
When I first went to this commune in 2024, I really hoped it could be an honest place of belonging and healing for me, I genuinely tried to make it work there. Like many others, I was drawn in by their marketing language around egalitarianism, cooperation, anti-racism, and alternative ways of living. 
</p>

<p>
Over time however, I began witnessing deep systemic contradictions between those ideals and the lived reality experienced by many marginalized people within the community, especially Black members and members of the “Racial Equity Team”. White members who were actual rapists got a slap on the wrist at worst, meanwhile members of color who aren't endlessly servile were under a microscope and scrutinized for any supposed violations of “policy”, subject to social shunning, hyperbolic and nasty communications, and character assassination, which we were expected to absorb gracefully.
</p>


<p>
Despite claims of egalitarianism, Twin Oaks is a community where white members saying racist things openly and in writing is considered much less controversial than members of color who push back (even politely). This was an environment dominated by white fragility, and fragility in general around any issue that requires privileged members to introspect or admit to having made mistakes/held erroneous assumptions.
</p>

<p>
As racial tensions escalated over the entirety of 2025, a few of us became increasingly involved in discussions around land reparations, structural racism, and the need for genuinely autonomous BIPOC-centered spaces within the wider intentional communities movement. Out of those conversations, I helped to envision a new land reparations project and healing-centered off-grid community rooted in ecological living, mutual aid, anti-authoritarian values, and long-term cultural transformation. For a long time, that vision for land reparations in order to create a new, better community represented the main hope I was still holding onto at Twin Oaks. Unfortunately, the hostility and backlash directed toward both the Racial Equity Team and myself became increasingly intense and emotionally unsustainable. Many of us experienced profound burnout, isolation, and mental health strain connected to those dynamics.
</p>

<p>
Then, in late January, the house I was living in at Twin Oaks caught fire while I was asleep inside, due to a faulty outdated electrical system. Thankfully, I escaped without physical injury, but most of my belongings were destroyed. With the community already at capacity, there was no longer anywhere at Twin Oaks where I could comfortably live. I was forced to go into debt to rent an Airbnb in Richmond, just to maintain some basic measure of stability and dignity. My attempt to protect myself from homelessness in the aftermath of the fire was subsequently weaponized against me, pushing me into the so-called “Twin Oaks debt money hole” and effectively sabotaging my membership. The humane response would have been for Twin Oaks to help cover my housing expenses after such a traumatic crisis, rather than deepen my existing housing insecurity. Instead, they seemed determined to exploit my vulnerability and make an already devastating situation even worse. A genuine community, possessing even a basic degree of empathy, compassion, or human decency, would have responded very differently to a house fire that continued to harm its own members. But Twin Oaks has shown me something quite different: a labor camp willing to treat human beings as disposable whenever they become inconvenient to the preservation of its status quo.
Nevertheless, there has been a silver lining. The distance and time away have allowed me to see the situation more clearly, beyond the insular bubble of what had become a filthy incubator of human despair. My experience mirrors accounts I have encountered from people who leave cults or abusive relationships, only to recognize after gaining some distance just how damaging the situation had truly become.
</p>

<p>
In the aftermath of the fire, I found myself navigating overlapping crises all at once: housing insecurity, emotional trauma, social fragmentation, burnout, and the collapse of the larger reparations effort we had been fighting for. During that period, I was desperately searching for safe and values-aligned places where I could land temporarily or rebuild longer term, but I quickly discovered how fragmented and inaccessible many existing intentional community networks actually are during moments of real crisis.
</p>

<p>
Ironically, it was only after leaving Twin Oaks and beginning a more nomadic phase of life that I really began to network more deeply through the wider BIPOC intentional communities movement, joining zoom calls and committees, visiting explicitly pro-Black healing spaces, intentional communities and organic farms (Heathcote Community, Soul Fire Farm, the New Economy Coalition, etc) that I slowly began finding meaningful support, invitations, and aligned relationships. Through the Healing Committee and other related spaces, I have now connected with people and projects across multiple states who share similar values and visions around land reparations, sanctuary, healing, racial justice, food sovereignty, and the creation of new communities.
</p>
